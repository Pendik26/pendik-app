# Chapter 1: Epithelial Tissue

Epithelium is one of the four basic tissue types, and it's usually the
first thing you're asked to name on a slide. The trick to identifying it
quickly is to answer two questions in order: how many layers, and what
shape are the surface cells?

## Step 1 — count the layers

- **Simple**: a single layer of cells, all resting directly on the
  basement membrane. Found wherever diffusion, secretion, or filtration
  needs to happen fast (capillaries, alveoli, kidney tubules).
- **Stratified**: two or more layers. Found wherever the tissue needs to
  resist abrasion (skin, esophagus, vagina).
- **Pseudostratified**: a common trap. It *looks* like multiple layers
  because nuclei sit at different heights, but every single cell actually
  touches the basement membrane — it's technically simple. Classic
  location: the respiratory tract, often with cilia and goblet cells.
- **Transitional (urothelium)**: unique to the urinary tract. Looks
  stratified cuboidal when relaxed, but the dome-shaped surface cells
  flatten out as the bladder or ureter distends.

## Step 2 — name the surface cell shape

- **Squamous** — flat, scale-like. Simple squamous lines vessels
  (endothelium) and body cavities (mesothelium); stratified squamous
  covers skin (keratinized) and lines the esophagus/oral cavity/vagina
  (non-keratinized).
- **Cuboidal** — roughly as tall as wide. Lines kidney tubules and small
  gland ducts.
- **Columnar** — taller than wide, often with a clear apical/basal
  polarity. Lines the GI tract (with microvilli for absorption) and, in
  its pseudostratified ciliated form, the airway.

## Step 3 — check for a surface specialization

Layers and shape are usually enough to identify a slide, but a complete
description adds a third detail: is the surface **plain**, **ciliated**,
or **keratinized/cornified**? Chapter 5 covers this axis in depth, along
with the junctions that hold epithelial sheets together, microvilli and
stereocilia, and how epithelium folds into glands.

## Putting it together

A slide description like "simple cuboidal epithelium" or "stratified
squamous, non-keratinized" is a complete, testable answer — layers first,
then shape. If you see cilia, think respiratory tract or fallopian tube.
If you see goblet cells, think a mucus-secreting surface (airway or gut).

## Key teaching point

Epithelium is always avascular — it gets its nutrients by diffusion
across the basement membrane from the underlying connective tissue. That
basement membrane is also the boundary pathologists watch for invasion:
a carcinoma that hasn't crossed it is still "in situ."
