# Chapter 2: Connective Tissue & Blood

Connective tissue is defined less by its cells than by what surrounds
them — the extracellular matrix. Blood counts as a connective tissue too:
its "matrix" is plasma, and its cells float free instead of being
embedded in fibers.

## The resident cells

- **Fibroblasts** are the workhorses — they synthesize collagen, elastin,
  and the ground substance that makes up the matrix. They're the most
  abundant cell in loose and dense connective tissue.
- **Macrophages** phagocytose debris and pathogens and present antigen to
  T cells, linking innate and adaptive immunity.
- **Mast cells** are packed with metachromatic granules containing
  histamine and heparin; degranulation drives allergic and inflammatory
  reactions.
- **Plasma cells** are terminally differentiated B cells with an
  eccentric "clock-face" nucleus, built to mass-produce antibody.

## Cartilage: three flavors

| Type | Matrix | Found | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hyaline | Glassy, type II collagen | Articular surfaces, trachea, fetal skeleton | Has a perichondrium (except at joints) |
| Elastic | Elastic fibers + collagen | External ear, epiglottis | Flexible, recoils to shape |
| Fibrocartilage | Dense type I collagen | Intervertebral discs, menisci | No perichondrium; heals poorly |

## Bone in three stages

Osteoblasts line the bone surface and secrete unmineralized osteoid.
Once matrix mineralizes around them, they become trapped as
**osteocytes**, sitting in lacunae and communicating through thin
canaliculi. Multinucleated **osteoclasts**, derived from the monocyte
lineage, resorb bone from the opposite side of the same remodeling
process — the two cell types are in constant balance.

## Blood as connective tissue

- **Erythrocytes**: anucleate, biconcave discs (~7.5 µm) built to
  maximize surface area for gas exchange.
- **Platelets**: anucleate cytoplasmic fragments of megakaryocytes,
  central to clot formation.
- **Granulocytes** (neutrophil, eosinophil, basophil) and
  **agranulocytes** (lymphocyte, monocyte) make up the white cell line —
  covered in detail on a peripheral smear, where granule color and
  nuclear shape are the two fastest identifying features.

## Key teaching point

When a slide question asks you to identify a connective tissue cell,
work through the same two cues every time: nuclear shape and granule
color. It gets you to neutrophil vs. eosinophil vs. basophil, and to
fibroblast vs. macrophage vs. mast cell, faster than trying to memorize
whole-field gestalt.
